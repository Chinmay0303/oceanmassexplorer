Ocean Water Mass Explorer: Interactive Water-Mass Selection in 3D

Problem statement
The ocean is made of water masses: large bodies of water that got their temperature and salinity at one place, for example in the North Atlantic, and then spread out. Below the surface, temperature and salinity change mainly when waters mix, so a water mass keeps its signature for a long time. Oceanographers plot each water sample as a dot, with temperature on one axis and salinity on the other. This temperature-salinity (T-S) diagram is the classic way to tell water masses apart [1]. Dissolved oxygen is a third property. Living things use it up once water leaves the surface, but analyses still combine all three [2,3].
These diagrams show no map, and a 3D map of one property hides how the properties combine. Our tool links the two. The user draws a rectangle or a curve on a T-S, T-O2 or S-O2 diagram of the World Ocean Atlas 2023 [8], and a 3D view, drawn by a GPU ray caster [4] in the browser, shows where that water is. A rectangle becomes a coloured volume and a curve becomes a surface.

Work completed
All eight proposed features and the novelty point are done, plus a few extras.
• Data: a Python script downloads 39 World Ocean Atlas files (temperature, salinity, oxygen; yearly and monthly; 1971–2000; 1 degree). Land and missing cells get the nearest ocean value, and a mask marks the real ocean. All files share one value range per property, so a saved selection means the same everywhere. The data is stored as 16-bit numbers and compressed from 341 MB to 162 MB.
• Time: yearly data has 102 depth levels (to 5500 m), monthly data 57 (to 1500 m). All 12 months stay on the graphics card, so the month slider, play and pause, speed, looping and blending need no loading.
• 3D view: for each pixel a shader sends a ray through one 3D texture (temperature, salinity, oxygen, ocean mask). The user can orbit, pan, zoom, stretch the depth, add clip planes along x, y and z, or view one depth slice.
• Colouring: temperature, salinity or oxygen can colour the ocean, with a value window, an opacity and a colour map (cmocean [9], Viridis or Cividis).
• Diagrams: T-S, T-O2 and S-O2, each with a log-scale histogram computed in the browser. A rectangle is drawn as a surface, a volume or both, through a transfer function [5] that gives colour and opacity from two properties at once. A curve becomes a fiber surface [6,7]: the ocean points whose values lie on the curve. We store the signed distance to the curve in a 2D texture, look for a sign change along each ray, and refine the hit by bisection.
• Selections: up to 8 rectangles and 4 curves from all three diagrams are drawn together. A list lets the user hide or delete each one, and a Select tool moves and resizes them.
• Picking: a click on the ocean shows its temperature, salinity, oxygen and position, marks the point in the diagram and pins it in 3D.
• Also: a quality setting, save and load of the whole setup, and a static GitHub Pages site built by a GitHub Actions workflow, so users only open a link.

Results and evaluation
We tested every feature on the real data with scripts that drive a browser (headless Chromium, software rendering), and again on the live website. To check the fiber surfaces we drew a thin T-S rectangle (a 1 °C band) and a curve, fixed the camera, and compared each image with one rendered at 2560 steps, our reference.

  Steps per ray    Rectangle pixels missing   Extra curve pixels   Colour error, before -> after bisection
  160 (Fast)       1.7 %                      5.7 %                6.83 -> 6.00
  320 (Normal)     0.6 %                      1.2 %                2.39 -> 2.19
  640 (High)       0.1 %                      0.4 %                1.03 -> 0.99

Colour error is the average difference in pixel colour on the surface. The first two columns come from our first version. Bisection left them almost unchanged and only lowered the colour error. A sharp V-shaped curve gave no holes and no false surfaces. The rest of the error comes from the step size, so High is close to the reference.
For picking, a clicked point differed from the data file at that place by 0.01 °C (temperature), 0.00 PSU (salinity) and 0.16 µmol/kg (oxygen). With five selections in three diagrams, hiding any one changed the 3D image (by 0.4k to 35.6k pixels), so all are drawn. We have not measured frame rates on real GPUs; all tests used software rendering.

Discussion
The fiber surface was the hardest part. A curve has no inside or outside, so for every spot in the diagram we store the distance to the curve, with a sign. When the sign changes between two samples along a ray, the ray has crossed the surface. This can fail in two ways: the sign can flip far from the curve and draw false surfaces, and a thin sheet of water can fall between two samples and leave a hole. So we refine each hit by bisection and drop hits where the distance is not near zero or the closest point is the end of the curve. In our tests we saw no false surfaces and few missing pixels.
We like the link between the diagram and the 3D view most. Draw a rectangle around a cluster in the T-S diagram and you see at once where that water lies in the ocean. Monthly playback and the oxygen diagrams can also show the yearly cycle and where the oxygen is low.
